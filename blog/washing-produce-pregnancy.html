How to Wash Fruit and Vegetables During Pregnancy

Use plain running water and practical preparation habits to wash fruit and vegetables during pregnancy.

Embryly Editorial Team
Editorial date: September 27, 2026 · Published October 14, 2026 · Sources checked October 14, 2026

You do not need a special bottle to wash an apple. For loose fruit and vegetables, FDA guidance recommends plain running water, with appropriate rubbing or brushing, rather than soap or a produce wash. Wash before peeling so handling the skin does not transfer dirt to the cut surface. Start with the FDA's produce-cleaning instructions.

Set up a clean starting point

Wash your hands before preparing food, and use a clean cutting board, knife and container. Keep produce away from raw meat and its juices during storage and preparation. Washing the fruit does little if it then lands on a board used for uncooked chicken.

Choose produce in good condition. Look for appropriate refrigeration when buying cut fruit or prepared vegetables. Remove damaged areas as recommended, and avoid treating a rinse as a way to restore spoiled food.

Match the method to the produce

Gently rub suitable produce under running water. For firm items such as a melon or cucumber, a clean vegetable brush can help. Remove outer leaves where appropriate. Dry with a clean cloth or paper towel after washing.

Even a fruit with a peel deserves attention before cutting. A knife passes through the outside before reaching the flesh. Washing the skin first is a small step that makes more sense than trying to avoid touching the fruit altogether.

Read the bag before washing again

For products labeled washed, prewashed or ready to eat, follow the manufacturer's directions. Additional washing is not always needed and creates another handling opportunity. Do not put ready to eat leaves into a sink that was just used for raw food and assume that counts as extra protection.

The FDA produce guide distinguishes preparation questions. Raw sprouts are not made equivalent to cooked sprouts by a longer rinse. If you are pregnant, follow the specific sprout recommendation rather than trying to solve it with the method for an apple.

Skip the cleaning experiments

Soap, household disinfectants and cleaning products are not food rinses. Use official instructions rather than an elaborate mixture from a short video. A method that removes visible dirt does not necessarily eliminate all harmful germs, and washing cannot make a recalled product suitable to eat.

Check a recall notice if one affects the exact produce you have. Follow its instructions. Do not treat washing as a substitute for discarding a recalled item.

Store for how you will use it

Keep perishable and cut produce refrigerated as directed. Prepare a manageable amount rather than letting a large bowl sit out while you finish other tasks. The CDC pregnancy table covers produce choices and specific cold-storage concerns.

Embryly can help identify a fruit or ingredient; it cannot tell whether you washed it. A clean board, running water and appropriate storage are the useful details behind a meal that looks simple in a photograph.
