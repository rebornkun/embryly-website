Salmon During Pregnancy: Cooked, Smoked and Canned

Understand the differences between cooked, refrigerated smoked and canned salmon during pregnancy, including preparation checks.

Embryly Editorial Team
Editorial date: September 11, 2026 · Published October 14, 2026 · Sources checked October 14, 2026

One word on a menu can describe several very different foods. Salmon can be a useful lower mercury fish during pregnancy when cooked appropriately. Raw salmon and refrigerated smoked salmon need different precautions from a cooked fillet or shelf-stable can. The FDA fish chart and seafood preparation guide answer different parts of the decision.

A cooked fillet is the simpler question

Choose salmon prepared according to current cooking guidance. Ask for it fully cooked when eating out, and use an appropriate temperature check at home. A seared surface does not tell you what happened in the thickest part.

Build the meal around what sounds appealing: potatoes and vegetables, rice and greens, or another combination that fits your preferences. The fish can be part of a varied eating pattern without becoming a special “pregnancy superfood” that you must eat every day.

Smoked does not always mean ready for this use

Cold smoked salmon and lox are often refrigerated foods eaten without further cooking. US pregnancy guidance recommends avoiding refrigerated smoked seafood unless it is in a properly cooked dish. The FDA ready to eat guide explains this category.

Do not judge only by “hot smoked” or “cold smoked” marketing words. Check the packaging, storage requirements and how you will prepare it. A refrigerated smoked product still calls for the pregnancy handling guidance. Shelf-stable canned smoked fish is a different product category from a chilled packet beside the deli counter.

Canned salmon can make lunch easier

Read the can's instructions, check that the packaging is intact and handle the contents appropriately after opening. Use it in a cooked dish or another preparation that fits current guidance and your needs. A shelf-stable unopened can does not mean an opened bowl belongs on the counter all afternoon.

If you are buying a premade salmon salad, the food has acquired another preparation and storage history. That is a separate question from whether salmon itself is lower in mercury. Making a fresh dish at home gives you clearer information about the ingredients and handling.

Think about the week, not one perfect plate

The FDA and EPA recommend variety among lower mercury fish rather than avoiding all seafood. Their chart can help you plan choices across the week. If you do not eat fish or have an allergy, discuss suitable nutrition options with your care team instead of forcing a food you cannot use.

Check the product behind the picture

A scan might recognize salmon, but a picture may not reveal whether it was raw, smoked, canned or reheated. Tell Embryly the relevant details when available, then verify the cooking and package instructions. The useful answer starts with the version of salmon in front of you.
